<Problem>
"The system must be highly available." How available? 99.9% allows 43 minutes of downtime a month; 99.999% allows 26 seconds. The difference in cost and engineering effort is enormous. And your service's availability can't exceed the product of everything it depends on. Reliability starts with **being precise about what you're promising** and understanding the math behind it.
</Problem>

## Reliability, availability, durability

- **Availability**: fraction of time (or requests) the system serves correctly. "Is it up?"
- **Reliability**: performs correctly over time, including correct results, not just responding.
- **Durability**: data, once stored, isn't lost (S3's famous "11 nines" is durability, not availability).
- **Latency** is part of reliability too: a page that takes 30 seconds is effectively down for users.

Measure availability from the **user's point of view**: successful requests ÷ total requests, as seen at the edge, not "the process is running".

## The nines and dependency math

<AvailabilityCalculator />

Two rules fall out of the math:
1. **Serial dependencies multiply**: four components at 99.9% each give ~99.6% end to end. Every hard dependency you add lowers your ceiling.
2. **Redundancy adds nines**, but only if failures are **independent**. Two replicas in the same AZ running the same bad deploy fail together.

## Designing for failure

Assume every component will fail. For each, decide in advance:
- **Redundancy**: N+1 instances, multi-AZ, replicas.
- **Isolation**: bulkheads and cells so one failure doesn't spread (one bad tenant, one bad shard).
- **Graceful degradation**: optional features turn off; core flows continue (lesson 5).
- **Fast detection and recovery**: health checks, alerts, automated failover, quick rollbacks. **MTTR** (mean time to recover) often matters more than MTBF (mean time between failures).
- **Avoid making soft dependencies hard**: if recommendations are down, the product page should still load.

<MentalModel>
A chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and a **longer chain has more links to break**. Reliability engineering is shortening the chain (fewer hard dependencies), doubling up the weakest links (redundancy), and making sure a broken link doesn't drop everything (degradation). Plus having a spare link ready to snap in fast (recovery).
</MentalModel>

## Blast radius and cells

Large systems use **cell-based architecture**: split users/tenants into independent cells (complete stacks), each serving a subset. A bad deploy or overload affects one cell's users, not everyone. Deploy changes cell by cell. AWS uses this extensively, and so does Slack.

<SeniorLens>
"Five nines" is rarely the right target. Users' own networks and devices are less reliable than 99.99%, so they can't perceive the difference, but they pay for it in slower feature delivery. Seniors negotiate targets **per user journey** (checkout: very high; admin reports: lower) and invest accordingly.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Availability is measured from the user's perspective, per request.
- Serial dependencies multiply; independent redundancy adds nines.
- Design for failure: redundancy, isolation, degradation, fast recovery.
- Pick targets per journey based on cost and user perception.
</KeyTakeaways>
