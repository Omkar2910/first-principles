<Problem>
3:12am. You're paged: checkout error rate 40%. Five people join a call; three start debugging the same thing, nobody updates the status page, the VP asks for updates every 5 minutes, and someone deploys a "fix" that makes it worse. Incidents are stressful and chaotic by nature. **Incident response** is the practiced process that turns chaos into coordinated action, and being good at it is a defining senior skill.
</Problem>

## Priorities during an incident

1. **Mitigate first, root-cause later.** Stop the bleeding: roll back, fail over, disable the feature flag, shed load, scale up. Understanding *why* comes after users are OK.
2. **Communicate.** Stakeholders and customers need regular, honest updates.
3. **Preserve evidence.** Note timestamps, take screenshots, save logs before they rotate.

## Roles (from the Incident Command System)

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Incident Commander (IC)** | Coordinates. Decides, delegates, keeps the big picture. Does **not** debug. |
| **Operations / subject-matter leads** | Investigate and execute mitigations in their area |
| **Communications lead** | Status page, stakeholder updates, customer support liaison |
| **Scribe** | Timeline of observations, decisions and actions |

For small incidents one person may hold several roles, but someone must explicitly be IC.

<MentalModel>
An incident is a **hospital emergency room**. Triage first: stabilize the patient (mitigate) before running every diagnostic (root cause). One doctor leads and assigns tasks, nurses handle specific jobs, someone keeps the family informed, and someone records what was done and when. Nobody performs surgery just to see what happens.
</MentalModel>

## A typical flow

```text
Detect   → alert fires (ideally SLO burn rate) or customer report
Triage   → acknowledge; assess impact & severity (SEV1: major user impact … SEV4: minor)
Declare  → open incident channel, assign IC, start timeline
Mitigate → what changed recently? (deploys, flags, config, traffic, dependencies)
           roll back / fail over / disable / scale / shed
Verify   → metrics back to normal? users OK?
Resolve  → close, communicate, schedule postmortem
```

The first question should almost always be **"what changed?"** Most incidents correlate with a recent deploy, config change, flag flip, certificate expiry, traffic shift or dependency change.

## Communication templates

> **[Investigating] 03:20 UTC**: Elevated checkout errors (~40% of attempts) since 03:05. Payments team engaged. Next update by 03:50.
>
> **[Mitigated] 03:41 UTC**: Rolled back the 02:58 payment-service deploy; error rate back to baseline. Monitoring. Root cause analysis to follow.

Be specific about impact and time, avoid speculation, and **always give the time of the next update**.

## Sustainable on-call

- **Actionable alerts only**: every page should need a human now; everything else becomes a ticket.
- **Runbooks** linked from alerts: what this alert means, how to check, how to mitigate.
- **Reasonable load**: if on-call regularly gets more than ~2 incidents per shift, fix the system or the alerts.
- **Escalation paths** and secondaries; nobody should be stuck alone.
- **Handoffs** with context; compensate and respect recovery time after bad nights.

<SeniorLens>
A great incident commander says things like: "**Alice, you own the database investigation. Bob, check the last three deploys. I'll update stakeholders at :30. Does anyone object to rolling back the payment deploy now?**" Calm, explicit delegation and decisions turn a frantic call into a fast recovery. This is a learnable skill. Volunteer to IC smaller incidents to practice.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Mitigate first, root-cause later; ask "what changed?".
- Assign an Incident Commander; separate coordination, investigation and communication.
- Communicate impact, status and next-update time regularly.
- Sustainable on-call: actionable alerts, runbooks, manageable load.
</KeyTakeaways>
