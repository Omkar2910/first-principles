<Problem>
"How long will this take?" You guess "two days" to sound competent. It takes eight. You don't mention it until the demo. Your manager loses trust; next time they check in on you constantly. Estimation isn't about predicting the future perfectly. It's about **communicating uncertainty honestly and updating early**, which is what makes you dependable.
</Problem>

## Why estimates go wrong

- Estimating only the **coding**, forgetting tests, review cycles, deploys, edge cases, meetings and interruptions.
- **Unknowns** not investigated (unfamiliar code, third-party APIs, data quirks).
- **Optimism and social pressure** ("it should be simple").
- Giving a **single number** with no uncertainty.

## A practical approach

1. **Break it down** into tasks no bigger than ~1 day. Big tasks hide unknowns.
2. **Identify unknowns and spike them first**: "I need 2 hours to check how the export library handles 100k rows before I can estimate."
3. **Estimate ranges with confidence**: "3–5 days; most likely 4. Biggest risk is the payment provider's sandbox."
4. **Include the whole lifecycle**: tests, review, deploy, verification, docs.
5. **Track actuals** against estimates for a few months. Your personal correction factor is real data.

<MentalModel>
An estimate is a **weather forecast**, not a promise. "70% chance of rain" is useful because it's honest about uncertainty, and forecasters update as new data arrives. A forecaster who says "definitely sunny" every day and never updates isn't optimistic; they're unreliable.
</MentalModel>

## Communicating progress

Good updates are **short, proactive and specific**:

```text
Export feature: Wed update
✅ Done: async job + S3 upload, tests passing
🔄 Next: email notification, UI status (Thu)
⚠️ Risk: big accounts (50k orders) take ~4 min to export; fine for async but I want to
   confirm the 7-day link expiry with legal. Asked Priya, waiting.
📅 Still on track for Fri, assuming legal confirms by Thu.
```

Rules:
- **Bad news early**: the moment you think a date is at risk, say so with the reason and options. Early bad news is a planning input; late bad news is a failure.
- **Bring options, not just problems**: "We can ship Friday without CSV formatting options, or Tuesday with them."
- **Close loops**: "Shipped ✅, monitoring metrics, follow-up ticket for X."

## Saying no (or not yet), constructively

When asked to take on more: "I can pick that up, but it would push the export feature to next Wednesday. Which is more important?" You're not refusing; you're making the tradeoff visible so the right person can decide.

<SeniorLens>
Senior engineers aren't better at predicting the future; they're better at **managing uncertainty**: they de-risk unknowns first, communicate ranges, and update early. Your manager doesn't need perfect estimates; they need to **never be surprised**.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Break work down, spike unknowns, estimate ranges with confidence, include the full lifecycle.
- Proactive, specific status updates: done, next, risks, date confidence.
- Share bad news early, with options.
- Make tradeoffs visible instead of silently overcommitting.
</KeyTakeaways>
