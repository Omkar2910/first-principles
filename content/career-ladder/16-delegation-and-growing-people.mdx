<Problem>
You became tech lead because you're the strongest engineer. So you take the hardest tickets, review every PR, and answer every question. Six months later you're the bottleneck: the team waits on you, nobody else understands the critical systems, and you're burning out. The hardest shift from senior to lead is realizing **your job is no longer to do the most work, but to make the team able to do great work without you**.
</Problem>

## Why leads struggle to delegate

- "It's faster if I do it." (True once. False over a quarter.)
- "They'll do it worse." (At first, maybe. That's how they learn.)
- "I enjoy the hard problems." (Valid, but hoarding them blocks others' growth.)
- Identity: being the expert feels safe.

<MentalModel>
A lead who does all the hard work is like a **parent who always ties their child's shoes** because it's faster. It is faster, every single morning, forever. Teaching takes longer the first few times, then the problem disappears and the child can do things you never imagined.
</MentalModel>

## Delegating well

1. **Delegate outcomes, not tasks**: "Own reducing checkout p99 below 500 ms" rather than "add an index to orders.created_at".
2. **Match to growth**: give each person work slightly beyond their current level (stretch with support).
3. **Provide context**: why it matters, constraints, what good looks like, who to talk to.
4. **Agree on check-ins**: more support early, less later. Ask "what do you need from me?"
5. **Let them own decisions**, including some you'd make differently. Intervene only for high-risk mistakes; let low-risk ones become learning.
6. **Give credit publicly**; take responsibility for failures as the lead.

A useful heuristic: **delegate your second-favorite problems first**, and occasionally your favorite ones too.

## Growing people deliberately

- **Know each person's goals**: what do they want to learn, where do they want to go?
- **Rotate ownership** so knowledge spreads: on-call, incident command, design docs, release management, interviewing.
- **Pair on hard problems** rather than taking them over.
- **Give regular, specific feedback**, positive and constructive ("In the design review, you handled the disagreement well by…"; "Next time, share the doc a day earlier so people can prepare").
- **Sponsor**: put people forward for visible opportunities and advocate for their promotions with evidence.
- **Reduce your bus factor**: if you'd be a single point of failure for any system or process, fix it.

## What you keep

Delegation doesn't mean disappearing. Leads typically keep: overall technical direction, the most critical design reviews, cross-team coordination, unblocking, and **some** hands-on coding (often on the critical path *less* than before, but enough to stay credible and close to the code).

<SeniorLens>
The measure of a tech lead isn't how much they personally ship. It's **what the team ships and how much the people on it grow**. If your team performs well when you're on vacation, and people from your team get promoted, you're doing it right.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Your job shifts from doing the most work to enabling the team.
- Delegate outcomes with context, appropriate check-ins and real ownership.
- Grow people via stretch work, rotation, pairing, feedback and sponsorship.
- Reduce your bus factor; keep direction, unblocking and some hands-on work.
</KeyTakeaways>
