<Problem>
Many engineers believe promotion comes from writing more code, knowing more frameworks, or putting in years. Then they watch a colleague with less experience get promoted first and can't understand why. Engineering levels are defined by **scope, autonomy and impact**: how big a problem you can be trusted with, how much guidance you need, and how much better things are because you were there. Once you see the ladder that way, growing on it becomes much more deliberate.
</Problem>

## The ladder at a glance

Titles vary (SDE1/2/3, L3–L6, Engineer I–III, Senior, Staff…) but the shape is consistent across companies:

| | **Junior (SDE1)** | **Mid (SDE2)** | **Senior (SDE3)** | **Tech Lead / Staff** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | Tasks | Features | Projects / systems | Multi-team problem spaces |
| **Ambiguity** | Problem and solution defined | Problem defined, solution open | Problem partly defined | Problem itself must be found |
| **Autonomy** | Needs regular guidance | Independent on features | Independent on projects; guides others | Sets direction for others |
| **Impact radius** | Own work | Own features | Team | Multiple teams / org |
| **Time horizon** | Days | Weeks | Months / quarters | Quarters / years |
| **Key question they answer** | "How do I build this?" | "How should this feature work?" | "What should we build, and how should the system work?" | "What problems should we solve, and in what order?" |

<MentalModel>
Levels are like **roles on a ship**. A deckhand executes well-defined tasks. A petty officer runs a section independently. An officer navigates a leg of the journey, adjusting for weather and coordinating sections. The captain decides where the ship is going and why. Each role needs real skill; what grows is the **scope of what you're trusted to decide**.
</MentalModel>

## The four dimensions that grow

1. **Technical skill**: depth (debugging hard problems, designing systems) and breadth (understanding adjacent areas). Necessary, but beyond mid-level, rarely sufficient alone.
2. **Execution and ownership**: delivering reliably, handling ambiguity, owning outcomes (not just tasks), including what happens in production.
3. **Communication**: writing and speaking clearly to engineers, product, leadership; making your work and reasoning visible.
4. **Influence and leadership**: making others better through reviews, mentoring, design guidance, and shaping decisions beyond your own code.

At junior level, technical skill dominates evaluation. By senior, all four matter, and **influence** is often what separates senior from staff.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Seniority = years."** Years give opportunities; what you do with them determines level. Some engineers have 10 years of experience; others have 1 year repeated 10 times.
- **"Promotion is a reward for past work."** Usually it's **recognition that you're already operating at the next level**, consistently.
- **"Senior means writing the most code."** Seniors often write less code but have more impact: unblocking others, preventing bad designs, choosing the right problems.
- **"Leadership requires a manager title."** Technical leadership (tech lead, staff) is about influence and direction, not reports.
- **"I'll get promoted if I just work hard."** Hard work on the wrong things, or invisible work, doesn't translate. Impact needs to be on things that matter, and visible.

<SeniorLens>
A useful self-check: **"If I disappeared for a month, what would get worse, and for whom?"** At junior level: my tasks wouldn't get done. At senior: the team's system would degrade and people would lose their go-to person. At staff: several teams would make worse decisions. Growth means expanding that radius.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Levels measure scope, ambiguity handled, autonomy and impact radius.
- Four dimensions: technical skill, execution/ownership, communication, influence.
- Promotion recognizes consistently operating at the next level.
- Use the self-assessment, then study the section for your next level.
</KeyTakeaways>
