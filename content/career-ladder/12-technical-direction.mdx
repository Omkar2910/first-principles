<Problem>
As a senior, you solve the problems in front of you very well. As a tech lead or staff engineer, you're expected to answer a harder question: **which problems should we be solving at all, and where should our system be in a year?** Without technical direction, teams drift: every project is locally sensible, but the architecture becomes incoherent, the same problems get solved three ways, and big risks go unaddressed until they become crises.
</Problem>

## Vision, strategy, roadmap

- **Technical vision**: where we want to be in 1–3 years and why. "All customer-facing services meet a 99.95% SLO with self-service deploys under 15 minutes."
- **Strategy**: the diagnosis of our situation plus the guiding choices to get there. "Our biggest reliability risk is the shared monolith database; we'll split payments and orders data first, because they cause 70% of incidents, and freeze new tables in the shared DB."
- **Roadmap**: sequenced, concrete steps with milestones, owners and checkpoints.

A good strategy says **what we will NOT do** as clearly as what we will. Saying no is how focus happens.

<MentalModel>
Technical direction is **city planning**. Individual builders (engineers) construct excellent buildings, but without a plan you get roads that don't connect, five water systems and no room for the hospital the city will need in five years. The planner doesn't lay every brick; they decide zoning, main roads and priorities so that everyone's building adds up to a working city.
</MentalModel>

## How to develop direction

1. **Diagnose**: gather data on pain points: incidents, slow delivery areas, cost, scaling limits, developer surveys, business plans. Talk to engineers, PMs and leadership.
2. **Identify the few things that matter most**: usually 2–4 themes, not 15.
3. **Write it down**: a strategy doc with context, diagnosis, guiding principles, choices, and what we're not doing.
4. **Socialize and iterate**: 1:1 conversations with key engineers and leaders before any big meeting; incorporate feedback; build co-ownership.
5. **Translate to a roadmap**: incremental milestones that deliver value along the way (not a 12-month rewrite with value at the end).
6. **Revisit regularly**: quarterly check: is the diagnosis still right? Are we making progress?

## Balancing product and engineering

Direction must serve the business. Effective tech leads **frame engineering investments in business terms** (speed of delivery, reliability customers feel, cost, risk) and integrate them into product planning rather than competing with it. A common pattern: a fixed share of capacity for platform/health work, plus engineering work bundled with the features that need it.

## Writing it: a lightweight strategy doc

```markdown
# Orders platform: technical strategy 2027
## Context        Where we are (with data): growth, incidents, delivery speed, costs
## Diagnosis      The core challenges: 1) shared DB contention 2) manual deploys 3) ...
## Guiding policy Principles: "new domains get their own datastore", "async by default for cross-domain"
## Actions        Q1: ... Q2: ... (incremental, each delivering value)
## Not doing      Rewriting the catalog service; adopting a service mesh this year
## Measures       p99 checkout latency, deploy lead time, incidents/month
```

<SeniorLens>
At staff level, **your writing is your leverage**. A clear strategy doc read by 40 engineers changes hundreds of decisions you'll never see. The best direction-setters spend a lot of time listening and diagnosing before writing, so that when they do, the strategy feels obvious and people adopt it as their own.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Vision (where), strategy (diagnosis + choices), roadmap (sequenced steps).
- Focus on the few things that matter most; state what you won't do.
- Diagnose with data; socialize 1:1; deliver incrementally; revisit quarterly.
- Frame engineering direction in business terms.
</KeyTakeaways>
