<Problem>
Every request your users send crosses coffee-shop Wi-Fi, ISPs and internet exchanges you don't control. Without encryption, anyone along the path can **read** passwords and tokens, or **modify** responses. And even with encryption, how does the browser know it's talking to the *real* `bank.com` and not an impostor? TLS solves both problems. You'll configure it, debug expired certificates at 2am, and decide where it terminates in your architecture.
</Problem>

## The three guarantees

TLS (the "S" in HTTPS) provides:
1. **Confidentiality**: nobody in the middle can read the data.
2. **Integrity**: nobody can modify it undetected.
3. **Authentication**: you're talking to who you think you are (via certificates).

## Two kinds of encryption, used together

- **Symmetric** (AES, ChaCha20): one shared key encrypts and decrypts. *Very fast*. Problem: how do two strangers agree on a secret key over a public network?
- **Asymmetric** (RSA, elliptic curves): a **public key** anyone can have and a **private key** only the owner has. Slow, but solves the key-agreement and identity problems.

TLS uses asymmetric crypto **only during the handshake** to authenticate the server and agree on a fresh symmetric key, then switches to fast symmetric encryption for the actual data.

<MentalModel>
Asymmetric crypto is a **padlock you hand out freely**: anyone can snap it shut on a box (encrypt with the public key), but only you have the key to open it (private key). TLS uses that slow padlock exchange once to safely agree on a **shared house key** (symmetric session key), then both sides use the house key for everything after.
</MentalModel>

## Certificates and the chain of trust

A **certificate** says "this public key belongs to `api.shop.com`", signed by a **Certificate Authority (CA)**. Your OS and browser ship with a list of trusted **root CAs**.

```text
Root CA (trusted, built into your OS)
  └─ signs → Intermediate CA
               └─ signs → api.shop.com certificate (contains public key, valid dates, hostnames)
```

During the handshake the server sends its certificate chain. The client checks: Is it signed by a trusted root? Is it unexpired? Does the hostname match? Then the server proves it holds the matching private key. An impostor can copy the certificate but not the private key.

## The TLS 1.3 handshake (simplified)

```text
Client → Server:  ClientHello (supported ciphers, key share)
Server → Client:  ServerHello (chosen cipher, key share), certificate, proof of private key, Finished
Client → Server:  Finished  +  first encrypted HTTP request
```

One round trip (TLS 1.2 needed two). With **session resumption / 0-RTT**, returning clients can send data immediately.

## Where TLS terminates

In real systems, TLS often ends **before** your application:

```text
Client ══TLS══▶ Load balancer / CDN ──(plain HTTP or new TLS)──▶ App server
```

- **TLS termination at the LB**: certificates managed in one place (e.g., AWS ACM), app servers stay simple.
- **End-to-end / mTLS**: re-encrypt inside the network too. Required by many compliance regimes and by zero-trust architectures. **Mutual TLS (mTLS)**, where *both* sides present certificates, is how service meshes (Istio, Linkerd) authenticate services to each other.

## Operational realities

- **Certificates expire.** Automate renewal (Let's Encrypt/ACME, cloud certificate managers). Expired certs are a classic, completely avoidable outage.
- Keep **private keys** out of source control and images; store them in a secrets manager.
- Disable old protocols (SSLv3, TLS 1.0/1.1) and weak ciphers.
- **HSTS** header tells browsers to only ever use HTTPS for your domain.

<SeniorLens>
In design discussions, "where does TLS terminate, and what's plaintext after that?" is a question that reveals security assumptions fast. And operationally: **every certificate should have an owner, automated renewal and an expiry alert**. Most TLS outages aren't crypto failures; they're calendar failures.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- TLS gives confidentiality, integrity and server authentication.
- Asymmetric crypto bootstraps trust and keys; symmetric crypto encrypts the data.
- Certificates chain up to trusted root CAs; the private key proves identity.
- Decide where TLS terminates; automate certificate renewal.
</KeyTakeaways>
