<Problem>
Your single database server is a **single point of failure**: if its disk dies, you're down (and maybe lose data). It's also a **throughput ceiling**: every read and write hits one machine. Replication copies data to multiple machines to fix both. But the moment there are copies, a new question appears: **what happens when the copies disagree?**
</Problem>

## Leader–follower (primary–replica)

The most common setup (Postgres, MySQL, MongoDB replica sets):

```text
            writes
App ───────────────▶ Leader ──WAL stream──▶ Follower 1
 │                          └─WAL stream──▶ Follower 2
 └── reads ──▶ followers (read scaling)
```

- All **writes go to the leader**, which streams its log to followers.
- **Reads** can go to followers to scale read traffic.
- If the leader dies, a follower is **promoted** (failover).

## Synchronous vs asynchronous

| | Synchronous | Asynchronous |
|---|---|---|
| Commit returns after… | at least one follower confirms | leader writes locally |
| Write latency | Higher | Lower |
| Data loss on leader failure | None (for confirmed writes) | Recent writes may be lost |
| If a follower is down | Writes may block | No impact |

Common compromise: **semi-synchronous**, where one follower is synchronous and the rest async.

## Replication lag and its anomalies

Async followers are typically milliseconds behind, but under load it can be seconds or minutes. That causes user-visible weirdness:

- **Read-your-writes violation**: a user updates their profile, refreshes, and sees the old version (read hit a lagging replica). *Fix*: read from the leader for a short time after a user writes, or for that user's own data.
- **Monotonic reads violation**: two refreshes hit different replicas; the newer data appears then disappears. *Fix*: stick each user to one replica.
- **Consistent prefix violation**: you see an answer before the question it replies to.

<MentalModel>
A leader with followers is a **teacher dictating to note-takers**. Everyone eventually has the same notes, but slower writers lag a few sentences behind. If you ask a lagging note-taker "what did the teacher just say?", you'll get an older answer. If the teacher leaves suddenly, the most up-to-date note-taker takes over, and anything said after their last note is lost.
</MentalModel>

## Failover is the dangerous part

Automatic failover sounds simple and isn't:
- **How do you know the leader is dead** and not just slow or partitioned? Timeouts are guesses.
- **Split brain**: the old leader comes back still thinking it's the leader, and two nodes accept writes. Systems use **fencing** (revoking the old leader's ability to write) and consensus (Track 4) to prevent this.
- **Lost writes**: with async replication, the promoted follower may be missing the last writes.

Managed databases (RDS, Cloud SQL, Aurora) handle much of this, but you still need to know the semantics: what's the RPO (data you can lose) and RTO (time to recover)?

## Multi-leader and leaderless

- **Multi-leader**: multiple nodes accept writes (often one per region). Low-latency local writes, but **write conflicts** must be resolved (last-write-wins, merge functions, CRDTs).
- **Leaderless** (Dynamo-style: Cassandra, Riak, ScyllaDB; named after Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper): write to N replicas, read from several, use **quorums** (`W + R > N`) so reads overlap with the latest write. Highly available; consistency is tunable per query. (Despite the name, Amazon **DynamoDB** is not leaderless: each partition's replicas elect a leader with Multi-Paxos, and only the leader accepts writes.)

<SeniorLens>
Two numbers every senior should know for each critical datastore: **RPO** (how much data can we lose?) and **RTO** (how long to recover?). And they should have **tested** failover, not just configured it. An untested failover is a hypothesis, and incidents are a bad time to find out it's wrong.
</SeniorLens>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Leader–follower: writes to the leader, log shipped to followers, reads can scale out.
- Sync replication avoids data loss but costs latency; async is fast but can lose recent writes.
- Replication lag breaks read-your-writes and monotonic reads; design around it.
- Failover is hard: split brain, fencing, lost writes. Test it.
</KeyTakeaways>
